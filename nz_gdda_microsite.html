Section 01 · Context

01Where we are

Two Cabinet papers have set a direction that's more ambitious and time-pressured than most peer-jurisdiction reforms. The how is still open — and worth engaging on.

Reading time: 3 minutes

The back-office mandate and Driving Down the Cost of Digital commit government to $13.9B of digital spend over ten years with a $3.9B savings expectation, and an ESM capability operational to agencies by July 2027. At this scale, marketplace listings and standards documents alone don't move the needle — the mechanism has to actually redirect investment.

GDDA is deliberately moving away from the “shared service” framing, recognising that agencies vary in capability, maturity, and legitimate need. The signalling toward clusters, common digital services, and eventual convergence rather than up-front unification is the right instinct. The sensitivity around cluster language — machinery-of-government versus digital-service clusters, agencies in multiple — reflects a maturity that central agencies elsewhere don't always bring.

What we sensed as still open — and worth engaging on. How does GDDA give agencies a fast, low-risk path to modern capability while preserving legitimate autonomy? How does it deliver Cabinet's cost mandate without funding a decade of duplicated consolidation projects? How does it engage the market fairly, while still expressing clear preferences?

What follows is a proposed answer to those questions — not a solution proposal, but an operating model. If any of it lands, we would be happy to develop it further with your team.

Section 02 · The Pattern

02The pattern we see fitting

Three architectural moves that connect. Each reinforces the others.

Reading time: 3 minutes
Move 01

A certification framework as the operating model

Standards, investment, and tiered signalling combined. GDDA publishes technology-agnostic interop patterns through the Enterprise Design Authority, certifies platforms against them in tiers (Reference, Approved, Assessing), and concentrates central investment on the certified list. Agencies pick; vendors compete; the mechanism through which Cabinet's cost-of-digital savings actually get delivered.

Move 02

A capability-domain Centre of Excellence

Structure within a structure. Horizontal governance (Exec Sponsorship, Strategy Office, Enterprise Design Authority, AI Enablement, Support & Enablement) applies across every technology. Under it, capability domains — Citizen Service, Content, Integration, Data Platform, Staff Service Management, HR, Finance, Productivity, Identity — each have a certified reference platform and certified alternates.

Move 03

One platform per end-to-end citizen-facing process

Reference-certified for Citizen Service Delivery owns intake, assessment, decision, fulfilment, notification, and closure — end to end. It integrates out to other systems for capability the process consumes (payments, records, finance) but never hands off mid-flight. This is the difference between architectures where a single platform owns the process end-to-end — patterns that repeatedly succeed in practice — and the two-platform “experience-front / delivery-back” split that has consistently failed across peer implementations.

The load-bearing move

The three ideas connect. The certification framework decides which platforms get invested in. The CoE describes how the invested capability is organised. The one-platform-per-process rule describes how agencies actually deliver on the invested capability. Take any one out and the others weaken. Together, they let GDDA say to agencies: "here is a certified reference stack, delivered end-to-end on one platform per process, and here is how it lowers your cost of getting there."

Section 03 · Certification Framework

03The certification framework

Not a compliance stamp. Not a vendor endorsement. An investment mechanism.

Reading time: Overview 5 min · Full 15 min

GDDA publishes technology-agnostic interop patterns, assesses platforms against them, and concentrates central investment on the platforms that meet the criteria — thereby lowering the marginal cost for every agency that chooses certified. Three components have to work together.

Component 01

Standards

Technology-agnostic interop patterns published by the Enterprise Design Authority — identity, canonical data, event contracts, security posture, sovereignty.

Component 02

Investment

Central spend on shared infrastructure per certified platform — reference blueprint library, shared talent pool, release pipeline, sandbox environments. The piece today's marketplace and standards regimes are missing.

Component 03

Tiered signalling

A market-legible label — Reference, Approved, Assessing, Divergent — that tells an agency what depth of centralised support it will get if it adopts a given platform.

The four tiers

Reference
Reference-certified

Deepest centralised investment. Comprehensive blueprint library. Shared CoE, talent pool, release pipeline. Fastest onboarding, lowest lifecycle cost.

Approved
Approved-certified

Conformant to the same standards. Blueprint library partial and growing; investment scales with adoption. A genuine second option.

Assessing
Under assessment

Vendor in flight through the certification lifecycle. Agencies may pilot but should expect divergent-cost profile until certification is granted.

Divergent
Non-certified

Agency choice permitted as long as the platform conforms to interop patterns and can participate in DPI. Agency owns full lifecycle cost.

The six criteria — what platforms are assessed against

Two are gate criteria — pass/fail. Four are stratifying — they determine whether a platform holds Reference or Approved status.

TypeCriterionDescription
GateInterop conformanceFederated identity, canonical data schema, event contracts, API standards, security posture. Can the platform participate in DPI at the level the EDA defines?
GateSovereignty & residencyWhere does the data live? Who has access? What's the sub-processor chain? Does the platform accommodate NZ Government data sovereignty and foreign-access constraints?
StratAccessibilityWCAG 2.2 AA as a floor. Native pathways for NZSL, Te Reo, Easy Read, assistive technology. Assessed at the platform level.
StratReference blueprint contributionCan the vendor deposit reusable patterns, configurations, accelerators into the shared library? Depth here stratifies Reference from Approved.
StratSkills availability in NZCan agencies hire people to run this? Local partner ecosystem, certified practitioners, training pipeline.
StratRoadmap & commercial transparencyPlatform roadmap visible to GDDA. Pricing model public. No differential pricing between agencies.

Three governance bodies

Technical body

Enterprise Design Authority

Publishes interop patterns. Runs conformance assessments. Grants and revokes certification. Vendor-neutral, independent from any single agency.

Commercial body

Investment Committee

Decides how much central investment goes to each certified platform based on adoption, criticality, and roadmap. Reports to Cabinet through GDDA leadership.

Consultation body

Vendor Advisory Panel

Certified vendors input on standards evolution and roadmap alignment. Consultative only — no decision rights. Keeps standards technically realistic without allowing vendor capture.

Precedents

Certification as a market mechanism has 20+ years of precedent across peer jurisdictions. This framework is a synthesis, not an invention.

  • UK Government — G-Cloud + Digital Marketplace + Service Standard. Tiered platform framework with published assessments and agency reuse. Directly analogous.
  • Australia — DTA Cloud Services Panel and the earlier Certified Cloud Services List. Federal-level tier system for cloud vendors with associated investment.
  • India — India Stack certified integrators (NPCI). Private-sector implementers certified to build on national public infrastructure.
  • United States — FedRAMP. “Certify once, reuse many” pattern for cloud security across federal agencies.
  • NZ Health & Education sector accreditation regimes. Domestic precedent for standards + investment + public tiering.

Why this delivers Cabinet's ask

The Cabinet paper Driving Down the Cost of Digital asks government to stop repeating investment across agencies for the same capability. Certification is the mechanism that operationalises that ask. Central investment concentrated on a small number of certified platforms replaces distributed spend on agency-by-agency rebuilds. Agencies choose from a transparent tier instead of running duplicate procurement processes. Vendors compete on published criteria instead of on relationships.

Section 05 · Digital Public Infrastructure Mapping

05How this maps to the Digital Public Infrastructure

Every component of the DPI target-state has a home in the certified reference architecture. DPI isn't replaced by the certified stack — it's populated by it. This section makes the mapping explicit.

Reading time: 4 minutes

The DPI target state describes an ecosystem-level layer — the shared standards, services, and integration surface that connects agencies to citizens. The certified reference architecture describes agency-level stacks — the platforms agencies use to deliver their services. The two operate at different scopes and complement each other. DPI defines what needs to exist across the ecosystem; the certified stack defines what an agency actually deploys to participate.

The load-bearing insight

DPI is populated by certified platforms conforming to shared interop patterns. It's not a separate thing to build in parallel — it's what emerges when the certified stack is adopted by every agency. Agencies consume DPI services (identity, payments, notifications, rules). Agencies publish to DPI events (application submitted, decision made, outcome captured). The result is that citizens see one government even when many agencies deliver.

What this shows. Fifteen DPI components; each maps unambiguously to one or two capability domains in the certified architecture. Nothing in the DPI target state is orphaned. Nothing in the certified stack is redundant. The distinction between the two is not competitive — it's structural: DPI describes what the ecosystem needs; the certified stack describes what each agency deploys to participate in that ecosystem.

A practical illustration. When a Ministry for Pacific Peoples applicant applies for a Toloa Fund grant: they sign in through DPI Identity Services (Domain 01); browse fund information via a page served by DPI's headless CMS (Domain 02); complete their application inside Salesforce, which runs eligibility rules published to the DPI Rules Library (Domain 03); the application publishes an event to the DPI Services Exchange (Domain 04); the decision, once made, is written to the DPI outcome feed which lands in the Data Foundation (Domain 05); a whole-of-Government dashboard consumes from the analytics layer (Domain 06); and payment goes through the central DPI Payments Management service. Every part of the journey passes through both an agency's certified platform and the DPI. The two don't compete — they combine.

Section 07 · One Platform Per Process

07One platform owns each end-to-end process

The recurring two-platform failure mode across government digital transformations — and the pattern that consistently succeeds when adopted.

Reading time: 4 minutes

The instinct that a customer-facing service should live on a “customer experience” platform and be handed off to a “service delivery” platform for the back-end is the single most common architectural mistake in government digital transformation. It has been attempted repeatedly across peer jurisdictions, and the seam between the two platforms is consistently where state, context, and accountability get lost.

A business process is one process. If you cut it across two platforms with different data models, workflow engines, and UX paradigms, the seam is where the state, context, and accountability get lost. What actually works is one platform owning the end-to-end process — intake, assessment, decision, fulfilment, notification, status, closure — and calling out to other systems for capability the process consumes.

The scope of “one platform” is the agency, not the ecosystem. This rule describes how a single agency delivers a single service — not how the whole of government fits together. Cross-agency integration and shared services are the job of the DPI. Salesforce is a good citizen of the DPI: it consumes DPI services (identity, payments, notifications, rules) and publishes to DPI events (application submitted, decision recorded, outcome captured). The process runs end-to-end within one agency's platform; DPI is what makes that process visible and composable across the ecosystem.

The principle

Salesforce owns the citizen-facing process end-to-end. Payments, records, finance postings, HR data, cross-agency data exchange are called out to via APIs and events. They are not handed the process. This is the pattern that has succeeded consistently in peer implementations — and its absence is the failure mode we've seen repeat across government transformations for the last decade.

Under this pattern, three different processes across the three ministries — Toloa Fund grant application for MPP, Nominations Service for MfW, accessibility complaint and provider oversight for Whaikaha — each run end-to-end on Salesforce as the anchor. All three exercise different process shapes (grants, matching, stewardship) on the same reference blueprint with agency-specific overlays.

The empty cells in other lanes are where a two-platform “experience-front / delivery-back” architecture would light up a second row and hand the process across — with all the state, context, and data-transfer problems that implies.

Section 10 · Operating Model

10Operating the certification body

How the certification framework runs day-to-day. RACI, lifecycle, cadence, and establishment path.

Reading time: 5 minutes

Standing up the certification body is a 12–18 month exercise, phased so first certifications can be granted in Year 1 while the framework matures. It fits inside GDDA's existing back-office mandate, using existing budget lines, without needing new legislation.

Certification lifecycle

Stage 01

Submit

Vendor lodges intent, conformance evidence pack, roadmap, pricing schedule. Rolling intake.

Stage 02

Assess

EDA runs conformance testing against published patterns. Two-stage: gates first, then stratifying criteria. Target 90 days.

Stage 03

Certify & invest

Tier decision published to register. Investment Committee funds shared infrastructure at the appropriate tier.

Stage 04

Operate & recert

Agencies adopt through shared CoE, blueprints, talent pool. Annual recertification. Sunset pathway when criteria no longer met.

Establishment — 18 months across three phases

Months 0–6

Phase 1 — Foundation

  • Publish interop pattern library v1.0
  • Establish Investment Committee TOR
  • Stand up Vendor Advisory Panel
  • Publish criteria & scoring rubric
  • Open rolling submission intake
  • First cohort of assessments in flight
Months 6–12

Phase 2 — Certification

  • First tier decisions published
  • Blueprint library scaffolding stood up
  • Shared talent pool established
  • Agency onboarding pathway published
  • First reference deployments (POC ministries) launched
Months 12–18

Phase 3 — Operate & extend

  • Full quarterly rhythm operating
  • First annual recertification cycle complete
  • Public annual report published
  • Extension to Crown entities considered
  • Framework v1.1 published

The full operating model covers decision rights across twelve certification decisions (with a complete RACI table spanning EDA, Investment Committee, GDDA Leadership, Vendor Advisory Panel, Vendors, and Agencies), the two-stage assessment methodology, investment allocation approach with tier scaling triggers, four tier-movement patterns (promotion, demotion, decertification, sunset), the appeals process with independent panel, public register & disclosure requirements, and the nested cadence of weekly / monthly / quarterly / annual operations.

Independence is designed in, not asserted. The Enterprise Design Authority makes technical assessments; the Investment Committee decides commercial investment; the Vendor Advisory Panel consults on standards evolution but holds no decision rights; the independent appeals panel can overturn certification outcomes. Appeals are heard by a body that is not the EDA and not the Investment Committee. That's the mechanism that makes the framework legitimate.
