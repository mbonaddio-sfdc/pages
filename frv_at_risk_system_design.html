At Risk Groups — a system that improves safety without building a hidden register

FRV's At Risk Groups function receives referrals about people and properties with elevated residential fire risk, provides advice, assessment and risk-reduction support, and can create a property-based advance warning for responding crews. This is a vendor-neutral conceptual and logical design for the technology that would support it — grounded in public evidence, explicit about what must still be validated with FRV.

Domain 1

Intake & Referral

Public, partner, health-provider and firefighter referrals converge on one auditable queue — never forwarded email as the system of record.

Domain 2

Specialist Case Management

Triage, risk assessment, advice, home visits, consent, partner coordination, follow-up and outcomes — the specialist workspace where humans decide.

Domain 3

Operational Alert Projection

A deliberately minimal, human-verified property-hazard alert to turnout/dispatch. No names, diagnoses or case notes.

The single highest-risk mistake would be to treat all referrals as one undifferentiated workflow, or to let a raw public report flow straight into dispatch. These are three linked-but-separate security and information domains with different users, purposes, urgency, retention and privacy exposure.

The controlled publication boundary

Everything in this design hangs off one idea: a human-governed gate between what is collected and what is shown to responding crews.

How to read this explorer

  • Historical documented by the Metropolitan Fire Brigade (FRV's predecessor) or an older source — useful for the operating model, not proof of current practice.
  • Inference a reasonable conclusion from multiple sources, requiring stakeholder confirmation.
  • Design implication a requirement or architectural consequence suggested by the evidence.
  • Open question cannot be answered reliably from public information — a decision point for discovery.
Status. This is a system-design hypothesis built entirely on public sources (inspected to 25 August 2026). It is strong enough to frame a design exercise and prevent major category errors. It is not strong enough to select a product, fix a final architecture, or estimate delivery without internal FRV discovery. It is deliberately vendor-neutral — no platform, product or API is named or assumed.

Scope, assumptions & design principles

What this system is for, what it explicitly is not, what we can rely on, and the principles that must hold no matter which architecture is chosen.

In scope

  • Omnichannel intake of residential fire-risk referrals (public web, firefighter, email/phone-assisted, partner agency).
  • Specialist triage, risk assessment, advice, home visits, smoke-alarm activity, PEEPs and presentations.
  • Consent and legal-basis capture, scoped by action.
  • A minimal, human-governed property-hazard alert projected to turnout/dispatch.
  • Renewal, review, discontinuation and occupancy-change management of alerts.
  • FRV↔CFA case/alert coordination for CFA-area addresses.
  • Records, audit, FOI/correction, retention and de-identified outcome reporting.

Out of scope

  • Emergency incident response and dispatch decisioning itself (this system informs turnout, it does not dispatch).
  • Building/commercial fire-safety regulation and false-alarm handling — separate FRV channels. Confirmed
  • Clinical treatment, property cleaning and enforcement — At Risk Groups is a prevention/advice function, not a clinical, regulatory or cleaning service. Confirmed
  • The Firelighting Consequence Awareness Program (Fire-CAP) — a referral/handoff, not shared case ownership. Design implication
  • General emergencies → Triple Zero (000).

Confirmed assumptions vs hypotheses

We can rely on (confirmed)

  • The function exists as the At Risk Groups Unit and receives multi-source referrals. Confirmed
  • Referral information may be used to give crews advance notice; hoarding notifications are recorded in the turnout system. Confirmed
  • Consent is not required to share for public/firefighter safety; consent is required for home entry, assessment or alarm installation. Confirmed
  • The current public intake is a Microsoft Form; the website CMS privacy statement may not cover it. Confirmed / Open
  • PDPA 2014, Health Records Act 2001, Public Records Act 1973 and VPDSS apply. Confirmed

Working hypotheses (validate)

  • The MFB pattern — address-based alert excluding occupant names, ~2-yearly renewal, discontinuation on move/threshold — broadly still holds. Historical
  • Service lines behave as distinct products with different workflows and completion criteria. Inference
  • Referral categories are not necessarily mutually exclusive or unique people/properties. Inference
  • Meaningful manual email/spreadsheet/re-entry work exists today. Inference

Design principles (non-negotiable)

Human approval before publishing, materially changing or removing an operational alert.
No raw public report flows directly to dispatch.
Consent is scoped by action — never one global boolean.
A no-consent public-safety basis requires recorded rationale and decision accountability.
Person data and property-alert data have separate access and retention treatment.
Every publication/removal has destination acknowledgement and an audit trail.
Property alerts have review and discontinuation workflows.
A resident move does not silently transfer person-related risk to a new occupant.
Integrations are idempotent, observable and safely recoverable.
Analytics use de-identified / aggregated data wherever possible.
AI may assist structuring or summarisation only with human validation, approved data governance and no automatic service or dispatch decisions.
Do not build a broad hidden register of vulnerable people — model property-centric hazards and person-centric needs separately.

Personas & access posture

Thirteen actors with materially different needs, urgency and data exposure. Access separates "need to administer the platform" from "need to read case content", and dispatch users only ever see the approved alert projection.

Access principles

  • Field-level or domain separation for health, behavioural and referrer-confidential information.
  • Dispatch users see only the approved alert projection — never the case.
  • Partner access is case-specific, time-limited and purpose-bound; partners do not get general system access by default.
  • Break-glass access (if supported) requires reason capture and review; sensitive-case access raises alerts and undergoes periodic entitlement review.
  • No shared mailboxes or generic accounts as the only accountability mechanism.
  • Reporting extracts default to de-identified data. Design implication

Architecture & options

Because key FRV internals are unknown, the design is expressed as a container view plus two viable architecture options with trade-offs, rather than a single prescribed build.

Container view (logical)

Two architecture options

Option A — Integrated case platform + thin alert projection

A single specialist case-management capability owns intake, case, person, property, consent and analytics. A small, isolated alert projection service is the only thing that talks to dispatch, fed through the approval gate.

For: fewer moving parts; one audit spine; simpler role model; faster MVP.

Against: tighter coupling risk; the projection must be genuinely isolatable for outage resilience; vendor lock potential.

Best when: FRV has (or will acquire) one platform and dispatch exposes a clean publish interface.

Option B — Composed services around a shared event/audit backbone

Separate intake, case, and alert-governance services communicate over an event backbone with an independent, append-only audit/record store. Dispatch publication is its own service with a durable queue.

For: strong domain isolation; the alert path can stay up independently; easier to swap components; clearer data-owner boundaries.

Against: more integration surface; higher operational maturity needed; more upfront design.

Best when: resilience/segregation are paramount and multiple existing systems must interoperate (incl. CFA).

Decision hinges on unknowns. The choice between A and B depends on whether an internal case system already exists, the turnout/dispatch interface, and the required RTO/RPO for the alert path. Both options honour every non-negotiable principle; they differ in coupling and operational cost. Carry both into discovery. Open question

Degradation & recovery behaviour

  • Case management down → active dispatch alerts remain available; controlled offline/manual intake opens.
  • Dispatch integration down → alert changes queue as signed/versioned events; backlog age is visible and escalated.
  • Public intake down → no impact on operational alerts; assisted phone channel continues.
  • On recovery → idempotent replay; reconciliation of manual entries; nothing silently lost. Design implication

Person ⟷ property separation

Lifecycle, provenance & correction (all entities)

Every entity carries: effective/valid-from & to dates; version history; provenance (source + who recorded it); verification status; correction history that preserves the original record and the authorised amendment; and deletion/retention controls governed by an approved PROV class — privacy does not authorise premature deletion of a public record. Confirmed

Minimal operational-alert schema

The alert domain carries only what a crew needs — and expressly excludes the rest. Every field must justify itself to responder safety.

Included — necessary to responders

  • Authoritative property/address identifier + geocode
  • Alert category codes
  • Verified, responder-relevant description
  • Operational implications — restricted access/egress, high fuel load, medical oxygen, inability to self-evacuate, aggression, hazmat
  • Source / verification confidence suitable for operational use
  • Creator, approver, approval time
  • Effective, review and expiry dates; status & version
  • Owning agency + contact for urgent correction
  • Removal / supersession reason
  • Publication acknowledgement from turnout/dispatch

Expressly excluded — unless FRV demonstrates specific necessity & lawfulness

  • Resident names
  • Diagnoses or clinical labels
  • Support-provider narratives
  • Case notes and assessment detail
  • Referrer-confidential information

Placing clinical labels into responder alerts is on the "do not do without explicit approval and evidence" list.

Data-quality controls

  • Authoritative address validation & geocoding
  • FRV/CFA jurisdiction determination
  • Duplicate detection by address, referrer and time
  • Structured risk codes plus bounded narrative
  • Observation date, and "unknown" distinct from "no risk"
  • Mutually-exclusive validation for "no risks" checkboxes
  • Source and verification status on every observation
  • Controlled vocabularies with versioning
  • Stale-data and occupancy warnings
  • Review of conflicting reports
  • Correction without loss of history
  • Attachment malware scanning & redaction if uploads are introduced

State machines

Five independent lifecycles. The load-bearing rule: case completion must not automatically remove a valid alert, and alert removal must not destroy the case record. They are linked, not fused.

1 Referral / Case

States are proposed, not confirmed FRV terminology — validate in discovery.

2 Service request

One case can hold several service requests, each running its own lifecycle to its own completion definition.

3 Consent / legal basis

Consent is scoped by action; withdrawal and expiry are first-class transitions. A no-consent path records a legal basis instead.

4 Operational alert — independent of case state

Draft → Verified → Approved → Active → Review due → Renewal/Change → Discontinuation → Removed → Archived. Removal is an explicit, versioned, acknowledged event.

5 Cross-agency (FRV↔CFA) transfer

Rejection and reconciliation are explicit states; acknowledgement is recorded, never assumed.

Coupling rule made concrete. When a Case reaches Completed, any linked alert in Active stays Active until its own Discontinuation path runs. When an alert reaches Removed, the Case and its audit history remain intact under the applicable retention rule.

Privacy, records & security

This function collects precise addresses alongside disability, cognition, mobility, medical-oxygen, substance and behavioural information — much of it personal and/or health information even when the resident's name is absent. Controls map to the Victorian regime.

Security controls & threat model

Baseline controls

  • PIA + security risk assessment before procurement/build
  • Formal information classification & Business Impact Level
  • Least privilege; RBAC/ABAC; strong identity + MFA; privileged-access controls
  • Encryption in transit and at rest; key management
  • Restrict bulk export; monitor anomalous access
  • Separate public intake / case workspace / alert stores
  • Service-to-service auth + schema validation on integrations
  • Tamper-evident logs independent of ordinary case editing
  • Contractual security/privacy/records controls on cloud vendors
  • Tested backup, DR and manual fallback; breach playbooks

Threat model (headline risks)

  • Over-disclosure to crews — a name/diagnosis leaks into an alert. Control: minimal schema + approval gate + projection-only access.
  • Hidden register drift — the hazard store becomes a de-facto vulnerable-persons database. Control: property-centric alerts, person data segregated, retention split.
  • Malicious/neighbour-dispute referral — weaponised intake. Control: verification before any contact or alert; no auto-contact on third-party address.
  • Insider / excessive access — browsing sensitive cases. Control: sensitive-case access alerts, break-glass with reason, entitlement review.
  • Stale alert harm — a crew acts on a hazard that no longer exists / wrong occupant. Control: review cadence, occupancy check, discontinuation workflow.
  • Integration replay / duplication — retries create duplicate alerts. Control: idempotency keys, correlation IDs, recorded acknowledgement.
Not for me to set. Final security classification and retention periods must be validated by FRV Records/Security — this design identifies the decision, not the value. Open question

Reliability & cyber resilience

FRV's December 2022 cyber attack disrupted email and systems and caused long-lived reporting and renewal problems — the 2024–25 hoarding-notification spike was partly a renewal catch-up afterwards. Confirmed. Resilience is therefore a first-order design driver, not an afterthought.

Isolation & availability

  • Isolation between public intake, case management and operational alerts.
  • Active-alert availability maintained during upstream outage.
  • Public intake failure cannot erase or corrupt operational alerts.

Safe publication & recovery

  • Signed / versioned queued publications.
  • Positive acknowledgement and idempotent retry.
  • Manual / offline intake with controlled reconciliation.
  • Backup restoration and rehearsed DR; point-in-time recovery.

Operational monitoring

Expose integration health, backlog age, failed synchronisation and stale alerts as live signals. Make reporting reproducible from durable event/history data — never solely from a mutable current-state table. Design implication

Outcome framework — four lenses

Demand & access

Referrals by channel, geography, source, service type, cohort; assisted/offline submissions; language & accessibility needs; rejected/redirected/duplicate/transferred; abandoned forms.

Timeliness & quality

Time to acknowledgement, triage, first contact, assessment, completed action; queue age & overdue by priority; duplicate rate & address-match quality; % with documented legal basis and current risk review; partner acknowledgement time.

Risk reduction

Working alarms confirmed/installed; exits/pathways improved; ignition hazards mitigated; escape plan/PEEP completed; support agency engaged; residual risk at closure/review; repeat referral / re-escalation.

Operational alert assurance

Active alerts by hazard/region; % acknowledged by destination; publication/removal latency; reviews due/overdue; alerts discontinued (moved / risk reduced); incidents at alerted properties + post-incident validation; false-positive/stale corrections.

Equity & safety measures (outcomes by geography, language, disability, tenure; privacy complaints; staff-visit safety; firefighter feedback on alert usefulness) use privacy-safe aggregation. Do not publish small-cell breakdowns that can re-identify vulnerable residents or addresses.

Delivery plan

Discovery-first, incremental, with the alert path proven early under realistic conditions. No product selection or final architecture until internal discovery closes the open questions.

Discovery & validation (mandatory before build)

Phase 1 — observe & inventory

Shadow intake→triage→assessment→publish→renewal→closure; map every form, spreadsheet, mailbox, database and manual handoff; trace one alert from referral to turnout display and later removal; document current metrics and reconciliation work.

Phase 2 — governance & risk

Run a PIA; classify each information asset and integration; identify applicable PROV retention/disposal classes; confirm legal bases and consent wording with Legal/Privacy; document FRV/CFA data-sharing responsibilities; threat model + BC analysis.

Phase 3 — co-design & validate

Co-design with At Risk staff, firefighters, Communications, CFA, referrers and people with lived experience; prototype the minimal crew alert under realistic response conditions; test outage / stale-alert / move / false-report scenarios; formal acceptance.
